# Research Questions and Hypotheses — Okinawa Preliminary Study

**R05 第4回沖縄本島中南部都市圏パーソントリップ調査 (2023)**

---

## Background

School escorting by parents accounts for 14% of morning peak trips in Okinawa (Okinawa Urban Transport Master Plan, 2026), in a city where private car trips represent 72.5% of all travel and public transit only 4.5% (Okinawa Urban Transport Master Plan, 2026). Escort rates vary substantially by school level — kindergarten 72.6%, high school 36.4%, university 8.0% — and among escorted trips, car accounts for 54–83% of the mode used (Okinawa PT Survey, 2023).

When asked why they escort, parents cite safety/crime concerns (code 02, combined — traffic and crime not separable) at 24.0%, while resource-based reasons (destination too far 18.8%, work trip-chaining 16.8%, need to be on time 12.1%, no suitable public transport 8.2%) account for 55.9% (Okinawa PT Survey, 2023). This gap suggests that escorting in Okinawa may be driven more by car availability than by fear — but it remains unclear how the two factors interact and whether observed mode choices reflect risk assessment or simply available resources.

It is also unknown whether household characteristics — particularly car ownership — shape school travel distance before any escorting decision is made.

---

## Role of This Study

This study aims to understand how safety concerns and household car ownership jointly shape escorting decisions and mode choice in a car-dependent urban context..

---

## Related Literature

**Distance and Upstream Household Effects**

- Yarlagadda & Srinivasan (2008) and Ermagun et al. (2014) use home-to-school distance as a predictor of escorting, but treat it as a fixed input without explaining why different households end up with different distances.
- Müller et al. (2008) builds a mode choice model for school trips but studies only a single school type and does not test whether distance varies across school levels or by household characteristics.
- Chillón et al. (2015), in a longitudinal study, find that travel distance increases markedly across school levels — median distance to primary school 1,370 m vs. 3,901 m to secondary school — but do not test whether household characteristics explain the variation.
- Wilson et al. (2010) demonstrates that school choice leads to substantially longer travel distances, but requires school-level data that is typically absent from PT surveys.

No study has directly tested whether car ownership shapes school travel distance before the escorting decision is made — suggesting that car ownership may act as an upstream factor in the system, not only as a determinant of escort mode.

**Safety vs. Security**

- Mammen et al. (2012) and Yarlagadda & Srinivasan (2008) treat safety concern as a single predictor of escorting, without distinguishing the source of that concern.
- Kamargianni et al. (2015) constructs a latent safety attitude variable but combines traffic danger and crime into one construct.
- Ferrell & Mathur (2012) is the first to separate safety (traffic danger) from security (crime) in a transit context, showing that the two respond to different policies — but this distinction has not been applied to school escorting behavior.

**Coping Appraisal and Threat Appraisal**

- Rogers (1975, 1983) frames PMT as a joint process in which Threat and Coping Appraisal together determine protective behavior.
- Floyd et al. (2000), in a meta-analysis of 65 studies, finds self-efficacy — a component of Coping Appraisal — to have the highest effect size (d⁺ = 0.88).
- Mashrur et al. (2022) confirms PMT pathways in travel behavior, but does not test whether Coping Appraisal can override Threat Appraisal under conditions of resource abundance.

This study therefore tests two gaps in the literature: whether separating safety and security as distinct Threat Appraisal components changes their explanatory power in school escorting, and whether car ownership as Coping Appraisal is strong enough to override the effect of safety concern on mode choice.

---

## Research Gaps

- Although distance is a key predictor of escorting, no study has examined how school level and household car ownership jointly shape travel distance before the escorting decision is made
- The safety vs. security distinction has not been applied to school escorting behavior
- No study has tested whether Coping Appraisal (car ownership) can override Threat Appraisal (safety concern) in escort mode choice

---

## Objective

1. To examine how school level and household characteristics — particularly car ownership — are associated with school travel distance

2a. To test whether coping appraisal (car ownership) predicts the probability of school escorting

2b. To test whether threat appraisal (safety concern) explains escorting motivation among parents who already escort their child

3. To test whether safety concern affects escort mode choice or is overridden by car ownership

## RQ1: School Travel Patterns
*(Stage 1 Proxy — in lieu of a School Choice Model)*

---

### Research Question

How are household characteristics and school level associated with school travel distance, and does car ownership remain a significant factor after controlling for other variables?

---

### Plain language

The deeper question behind RQ1 is: why do families send their children to distant schools when closer ones exist? That is the school choice question to be modeled formally in Bangkok. But before going there, a more foundational question must be answered first: **do car-owning households end up with longer school travel distances than non-car-owning households?**

If yes, it means having a car enables families to access schools further away — the car expands the accessible school set. Households without a car are effectively constrained to nearby schools. If no difference is found, distance is explained by other factors such as school level, income, or household structure.

H1a and H1b provide context — distance increases with school level, and zone-crossing rates increase as children age. These establish the baseline patterns that H1c is tested against. **H1c is the main hypothesis of RQ1.**

Okinawa is well-suited to testing this because of its high car dependency, which makes the effect of car ownership on school access visible without being confounded by multiple competing modes — unlike Bangkok, where car, motorcycle, and transit all overlap.

If Okinawa confirms that "car ownership → access to more distant schools," Bangkok can then ask whether transit or motorcycle replicates this enabling role — whether non-car households with good transit access can reach equally distant schools. That is the link between the two studies.

Limitation: the dataset contains no school identifiers — only origin and destination zones. Distance and zone-crossing are therefore used as proxies for school choice. The distance estimated in RQ1 becomes an input variable in RQ2.

---

### Data available and sufficient

- B-zone origin (home) + B-zone destination (school) → zone-to-zone distance can be computed
- School level (grade) for every student
- Trip mode
- No school name, but distance and zone-crossing are adequate proxies

---

### Hypotheses

**H1a:** School travel distance increases by school level (kindergarten < elementary < high school < university)

> *(Chillón et al., 2015)*

**H1b:** Zone-crossing rate increases with school level — most elementary students attend a school within their home zone, while high school students are more likely to cross into another zone — reflecting that older students travel further and attend schools outside their immediate zone at higher rates. In the Japanese context, this pattern is structurally enforced: elementary and junior high school enrollment is governed by residential catchment zones (通学区), leaving virtually no zone-crossing, while senior high school admission is based on entrance examinations open across a wider geographic area, making zone-crossing the norm.

**H1c:** Car-owning households have longer school travel distances than non-car-owning households — having a car enables travel to more distant schools without needing to rely on a nearby school, meaning car ownership exerts an upstream effect on the escorting system before the escort decision itself occurs.

Limitation: the dataset cannot distinguish whether longer distances result from "choosing a distant school" vs. "living far from any nearby school" — but the correlation between car ownership and distance is sufficient for the upstream analysis.

---

### Analyses planned

- Distance distribution by school level (H1a)
- Zone-crossing rate by school level (H1b)
- OD matrix — home B-zone → school B-zone (spatial clustering)
- Mode split by school level (descriptive)
- OLS regression: `distance ~ car_ownership + school_level + HH_size + mother_employment + income` (H1c)

## RQ2: Safety, Escorting, and Mode Choice
*(Stage 2 — Main Research Question)*

---

### Theoretical Framework: Protection Motivation Theory (PMT)

PMT proposes that behavior is jointly determined by two appraisal processes (Rogers, 1975, 1983; Floyd et al., 2000):

- **Threat Appraisal:** How dangerous is it for a child to travel alone? If perceived risk is high → motivation to escort increases.
- **Coping Appraisal:** Does the household have the resources to escort? (car ownership, time, available adults)

In Okinawa: high car ownership + low crime → Coping Appraisal is expected to dominate Threat Appraisal.
In Bangkok: heavy traffic + higher crime → Threat Appraisal is expected to play a stronger role (Mammen et al., 2012; Sattanon & Upala, 2018; Prakayaphun et al., 2023).

RQ2 asks how these two forces operate in Okinawa and whether safety concern actually changes mode choice (Mashrur et al., 2022).

---

### Research Question

How do safety concerns and household coping resources (particularly car ownership) jointly determine school escorting behavior and transport mode choice in a highly car-dependent city?

---

### Sub-RQ 2a: Escort Probability

#### Question

Does coping appraisal (car ownership) predict whether parents escort their child to school? And among those who do escort, does threat appraisal (safety concern) explain their motivation — over and above resource-based reasons?

#### Plain language

Before asking "escorted by what mode," we need to know "who gets escorted." This sub-RQ builds a binary logit model to test how fear, car ownership, distance, and household structure each contribute to the probability of escorting.

#### Data available

- `q6_escorting_flag` (1/0) from Supplementary Survey — dependent variable
- `car_ownership` — Coping Appraisal proxy
- Q6 code 02 (safety/crime concerns, combined) → single Threat Appraisal dummy; traffic and crime not separable in this dataset
- `employment_student_status` for all household members → mother's employment status
- `household_size`, number of children in household
- Distance from zone OD (carried over from RQ1)
- School level, child's gender

#### Hypotheses

**H2a (Stage 1):** Car ownership (Coping Appraisal) significantly increases escort probability, and younger school level (proxy for higher perceived vulnerability) also increases escort probability.

> *(Vovsha & Petersen, 2005; Yarlagadda & Srinivasan, 2008; Ermagun et al., 2014)*

**H2b (Stage 1):** Car ownership has a larger effect on escort probability than school level — having a car makes escorting logistically possible regardless of the child's age or perceived vulnerability.

> *(Rogers, 1983; Floyd et al., 2000; Vovsha & Petersen, 2005; Ermagun et al., 2014)*

**H2c (Stage 2):** Among parents who already escort their child, those who cite safety/crime concern (code 02) as the reason are more likely to have younger children and no car — suggesting that threat appraisal dominates when coping resources are limited.

> *(Mammen et al., 2012; Sattanon & Upala, 2018; Yarlagadda & Srinivasan, 2008; Ferrell & Mathur, 2012)*

**Note:** Safety concern (code 02) is only observed for escorting parents (flag=1) — it cannot be used as a predictor in Stage 1. The two-stage design is required because the Supplementary Survey asks *why* parents escort, but does not ask non-escorting parents about their safety perceptions.

---

### Sub-RQ 2b: Mode Choice and Safety *(Core Finding)*

#### Question

Does safety concern influence the mode chosen for school escorting, or is it overridden by car ownership?

#### Plain language

This is the core of the study. A critical point: car-owning and non-car-owning households are in structurally different situations. A household without a car doesn't "choose not to use a car" — the car simply isn't available. Therefore the model uses MNL with availability conditions, where "car" is only in the choice set for households that own one. This way, safety concern is tested across all households — non-car households may show safety effects on remaining modes (e.g., accompanying on foot instead of letting the child walk alone), while car-owning households may show no safety effect on mode because the car already dominates.

**Key limitation:** Transit is near-zero in Okinawa escort trips, so mode choice can only be tested as car vs. walk. Car vs. transit analysis will be conducted in Bangkok, where transit is a real option.

#### Data available

- `trip_mode` of the escorter (car driver, passenger, walk) — dependent variable
- `safety_concern` dummy from Q6 code 02 — Threat Appraisal proxy (combined safety/crime; not separable)
- `car_ownership` — determines availability condition for "car" mode
- Limitation: transit trips near-zero → MNL limited to car vs. walk

#### Hypotheses

**H2e:** Car ownership is the dominant predictor of escort mode — if a car is available, it will be used regardless of other stated reasons. This is consistent with Coping Appraisal, in which resource availability determines behavior more strongly than perception.

> *(Rogers, 1983; Floyd et al., 2000; Vovsha & Petersen, 2005; Ermagun et al., 2014)*

**H2f:** The effect of safety concern on mode choice varies by available choice set — in car-owning households, safety does not affect mode (car dominates Threat Appraisal), while in non-car households, safety may influence choices among remaining modes.

> *(Ben-Akiva & Lerman, 1985; Train, 2009; Yarlagadda & Srinivasan, 2008; Kamargianni et al., 2015; Ferrell & Mathur, 2012; De Martini et al., 2026)*

**H2g:** The safety coefficient in the utility of "car" (among car-owning households) is not statistically significant, indicating that Coping Appraisal dominates Threat Appraisal in a context where car is the default mode and transit is near-absent. This result is context-specific and is expected to differ in Bangkok.

> *(Rogers, 1983; Floyd et al., 2000; Mashrur et al., 2022; Kamargianni et al., 2015; Bhat & Dubey, 2014; De Martini et al., 2026)*

#### Analyses planned

- MNL with mode availability conditions — car only in choice set for car-owning households (Ben-Akiva & Lerman, 1985; Train, 2009)
- Binary logit (car vs. walk) for 1+ car households only — test H2e and H2f
- Compare safety coefficient across car-owning vs. non-car households — test H2f
- Document limitation: transit near-zero → car vs. transit to be tested in Bangkok only

## Limitations to Declare in the Paper

1. **School choice:** Travel distance and zone-crossing are used as proxies only — not a formal school choice model, due to the absence of school identifiers in the dataset. (Wilson et al., 2010; Müller et al., 2008)
2. **PMT measurement:** Q6 uses binary checkboxes, not Likert scales — PMT functions as an organizing framework only, not a structural latent variable model. Full ICLV will be implemented in the Bangkok study. (Bhat & Dubey, 2014; Kamargianni et al., 2015)
3. **Safety vs. Security separation:** Q6 code 02 combines traffic safety and crime into a single response — the Ferrell & Mathur (2012) separation is not possible with this dataset. A single binary dummy is used for Threat Appraisal. This is declared as a data limitation; the Bangkok SP survey will be designed to separate the two.
4. **Transit near-absent:** Mode choice analysis is limited to car vs. walk in Okinawa — car vs. transit will only be testable in Bangkok. (Ong & Sanit, 2024)
5. **Motorcycle risk perception (Bangkok-forward):** In contexts where motorcycles are ubiquitous, parents may underestimate motorcycle risk due to familiarity bias. This will be addressed in the Bangkok SP survey design. (Raftery, 2023)

## Analysis 1: School Travel Distance Patterns
*(Addressing RQ1)*

**Background**

Since the dataset contains no school identifiers, a formal school choice model cannot be estimated. Travel distance and zone-crossing are used as behavioral proxies to examine spatial patterns by school level and household characteristics. Distance is an established predictor of escorting behavior and mode choice (Ermagun et al., 2014; Yarlagadda & Srinivasan, 2008), and open school choice has been shown to substantially increase travel distances (Wilson et al., 2010).

**Data Availability**

| Variable | Source | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| B-zone origin + destination | Person Trip Survey | Used to compute distance and zone-crossing |
| School level / student type | Person Trip Survey | Derived from occupation code |
| Car ownership | Household Survey | Number of cars in household |
| HH size, mother's employment | Household Survey | — |
| Income (年収) | Household Survey | Ordinal 1–10, ~21% missing |

**Methodology**

- **H1a** Distance distribution by school level — descriptive statistics (mean, median, IQR) + box plot + One-way ANOVA (F-statistic, p-value)
  - One-way ANOVA rather than t-test: H1a has 4 groups (kindergarten, elementary/JHS, high school, university). A t-test handles only 2 groups; running multiple t-tests across pairs inflates Type I error. ANOVA tests all groups simultaneously in one test. With the large N of a PT survey, ANOVA is robust to non-normality via the Central Limit Theorem.
- **H1b** Zone-crossing rate by school level — cross-tabulation + Chi-square test
  - Zone-crossing defined as: home B-zone ≠ dest B-zone
  - Chi-square tests whether zone-crossing (binary) and school level (categorical) are independent
- **H1c** OLS regression: `travel_time ~ car_ownership + school_level + HH_size + income`
  - OLS rather than MNL: the dependent variable is travel time (continuous), not a discrete mode choice — MNL is reserved for RQ2.
  - Filter: trip_purpose = '02'; employment_student_status ∈ {8,9,10,11}; exclude travel_time ≥ 999 (sentinel value = missing/unknown)

*Data source: R05 Okinawa PT Survey 2023 | HH Survey + PersonTrip + Supplementary Q6*

## Analysis 2: Escorting Decision and Mode Choice
*(Addressing RQ2a and RQ2b)*

**Data Availability**

| Variable | Source | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| Escort flag | Supplementary Survey | `q6_escorting_flag`: 1 = escorts child, 2 = does not; 922 escorting respondents |
| Escort mode | Person Trip Survey | `mode1_mode`: recoded — 07/08/80 = car, 01 = walk; transit near-zero in escort trips |
| Safety/crime concern | Supplementary Survey | `q6_child1_escort_reason = '02'`; cited by 24.0% of escorting respondents; traffic and crime **not separable** — single binary dummy |
| Car ownership | Person Trip Survey | `owned_has_car`: 1 = owns car (binary) |
| Number of cars | Person Trip Survey | `owned_num_cars`: 0, 1, 2, 3+ |
| Escorter sex | Person Trip Survey | `sex`: 1 = Male, 2 = Female; Female = 64.2% of escort trips |
| Household size | Person Trip Survey | `hh_size_excl_under5` |
| School level of child | Supplementary Survey | `q6_child1_school_dest`: 1 = Kindergarten, 2 = Elementary, 3 = JHS, 4 = High school, 5 = University |
| Child sex | Person Trip Survey | Derived via `escorted_hh_member_no_1` → joined to `hh_member_person_no` + `sex` |
| Travel time (distance proxy) | Person Trip Survey | `mode1_travel_time_min`; carried from RQ1 — exclude ≥ 999 |
| Annual income | Person Trip Survey | Ordinal 1–8; exclude 9 = unknown (~21% missing) |

**Methodology**

**Stage 1 — Binary Logit: Escort Probability (RQ2a, Part 1)**

Sample: Supplementary Survey respondents with at least one school-age child in the household (employment/student status codes 8–11 in the PT file), n = 1,353 (616 escorting, 737 non-escorting).

Model:

`escort (1/0) ~ car_ownership + school_level + escorter_sex + hh_size + child_sex + income + income_unknown`

- `car_ownership` (`owned_has_car`) — Coping Appraisal proxy; expected positive
- `school_level` (`q6_child1_school_dest`, 1–5) — Threat Appraisal proxy; expected negative (younger child → higher probability)
- `escorter_sex`, `hh_size`, `child_sex` — control variables
- `income` (ordinal 1–8) — control; ~21% missing
- `income_unknown` (dummy = 1 if income missing) — retains full sample; avoids listwise deletion

Safety concern (`q6_child1_escort_reason == '02'`) is **not included** in Stage 1 — it is only observed for flag=1 respondents and cannot be used to predict escort probability across both groups.

---

**Stage 2 — Binary Logit: Safety vs. Resource Motivation (RQ2a, Part 2)**

Sample: Escorting parents only (flag=1), n = 922.

Model:

`safety_concern (1/0) ~ car_ownership + school_level + escorter_sex + hh_size + child_sex + income + income_unknown`

- `safety_concern` = 1 if `q6_child1_escort_reason == '02'`, else 0 (resource-based or other reason)
- Same predictors as Stage 1
- Tests whether parents who lack a car or have younger children are more likely to cite safety as the reason for escorting (H2c)

---

**RQ2b — MNL with Availability Conditions: Escort Mode Choice**

Sample: Escorting parents (flag=1) with valid mode data, n ≈ 616 (after matching to PT trips).

Model: mode (car / walk) as dependent variable

- Car is in the choice set only for households with `owned_has_car = 1`
- Tests whether `safety_concern` coefficient on car utility is significant
- Transit excluded: near-zero frequency in escorted trips in Okinawa

*Data source: R05 Okinawa PT Survey 2023 | PersonTrip + Supplementary Q6*
